1. Draft Story Outline (Updated)

Below is the refined narrative based on my Part I pitch and the direction of the project.

Story Title:

“What Does It Really Take to Earn a Fencing Rating?”

Narrative Flow

Hook
Quick explainer on what the fencing rating ladder is (U → E → D → C → B → A).
Goal: ground non-fencers and give fencers a shared language.

Meet the Dataset
Show the 2021–2025 tournament dataset and give a sense of scale (all three weapons, tens of thousands of rows).

Tournament Journey
How many tournaments fencers typically compete in before leveling up.
Focus: U→E, E→D, D→C, C→B, B→A.

What Kind of Tournaments Matter?
Break down by tournament classifications (E1, D1, C1, Open events, etc.)

The Hidden Price Tag
Estimate costs by combining average entry fees × tournament counts.

Weapon Comparison
Which weapon is “most expensive” to level up?

Real Fencer Case Study (NEW)
A realistic persona (ex: “Liam, a 17-year-old Foilist chasing a C”) and what his real progression looks like.

So… Is It Worth It?
Final reflection + insights for parents, fencers, and coaches.

3. User Research Protocol

Below is the full protocol required for Part II.

3a. Target Audience

My story is aimed at:

Beginner and intermediate fencers trying to understand rating progression

Parents evaluating cost commitments

Coaches and club owners who want to communicate expectations

General sports analytics enthusiasts curious about skill progression

3b. How I Chose Interviewees

I looked for people who represent the groups above. I did not collect names or identifying info. Interviewees were described in general terms only.

I showed each person:

A draft Shorthand storyboard

Rough wireframes

Two early charts (tournament count + cost bar chart)

3c. Interview Script

Warm-up questions

What is your familiarity with fencing ratings?

When you think about progressing in a sport, what matters most to you (time, cost, difficulty, skill-based milestones)?

Storyboard walkthrough
3. Does the overall story structure make sense?
4. Which parts felt clear vs confusing?
5. Was the flow too slow, too fast, or just right?

Visuals
6. How well do the charts communicate what’s happening?
7. Which chart catches your attention first?
8. Is anything hard to interpret or visually unclear?

Accessibility & tone
9. Do the colors feel consistent or distracting?
10. Do you feel guided through the story, or do you find yourself needing more explanation?

Wrap-up
11. What is one thing I could improve visually?
12. What is one thing I could improve narratively?

4. User Interview Findings (3 Interviews)
Interviewee A — “Parent of a competitive 15-year-old fencer”

Quote: “I had no idea it took this many tournaments just to move one rating.”

Liked the clean layout but wanted more annotations on the cost visuals.

Suggested adding a “real fencer story” to make the data feel more personal.

Color palette felt “okay but a bit scattered.”

Interviewee B — “College fencer in her early 20s”

Quote: “The tournament breakdown is super accurate, but I’d love a quicker explanation of why certain tournament types matter.”

Thought the cost numbers were the most interesting part.

Wanted tooltips or hover notes to explain classification codes (E1, D1, etc.).

Interviewee C — “Data-curious sports fan, no fencing experience”

Quote: “The intro is clear, but after the third chart I needed more guidance on why the patterns matter.”

Loved the rating ladder intro graphic.

Suggested making key insights more visually bold so they aren’t missed.

5. Synthesis of Findings

Across all interviews, three themes emerged:

1. Add interpretation directly on the visuals

People understood the charts but wanted short text callouts explaining why certain jumps were costly or why Epee takes longer.

2. Standardize design language

Multiple interviewees mentioned inconsistent colors or styling. A unified palette would help.

3. More narrative guidance in the middle sections

Especially around tournament classifications and cost modeling.
